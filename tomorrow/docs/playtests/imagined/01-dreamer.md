# Tomorrow, imagined: the Dreamer

_One imagined Nell plays "Tomorrow" without the parser's limits. At each of the seven moments she tries whatever a real seventeen-year-old in a time loop would try, and this file imagines what happens, in the game's voice._

**How to read this.**
- **IN GAME** means it already exists, and the branch or free reply is named. **PARTLY** means the words already go somewhere, just not here. **NEW** means it doesn't exist yet.
- Everything marked IN GAME or PARTLY was checked with the play harness on the second Tuesday or later. The result is quoted where it matters.
- Proposed facts are in `code` and marked NEW. Details that would become canon are marked _(proposed canon)_.
- Blockquotes are game prose (second person, present tense). Chains are Nell (first person).

---

## Who this Nell is

> The first thing I did, the second Tuesday, was check if the stain had moved.
>
> It hadn't. Same not-Ohio. Same corner peeling.
>
> Okay. So it has rules. Everything has rules. Curses have rules, fairy tales have rules, Calder has rules: eleven streets, don't ask for things back, Dot starts your grilled cheese when the bell rings. Find the rules, find the door.
>
> That's what I told myself. A loop is a puzzle. A puzzle isn't my fault.

She's the Nell who treats the loop as a place. She tests its edges like a kid testing a frozen pond. What survives the night? How far does the bus go? Does the stain change? Who keeps saying *you*? She talks to objects, and to the narrator. She looks at everything, so she finds the game's seams faster than anybody.

**What drives her.** Magical thinking is one more kind of regret. If the loop is a spell, then there's a trick that undoes it, and the trick isn't her. Every experiment she runs comes back with the same answer, just dressed differently: **the loop's rules are her rules.** What she learns survives the night. What she does doesn't. The road goes around because she does. The stain is shaped like the town because the town is shaped like her.

**How she overthinks.** In images, not arguments. Where another Nell asks "what should I have said?", she asks "what does the stain *mean*?" Her chains have the same "but…" steps as the author's, but each step reaches for a symbol: the bus, the tower, the porch light, the photo strip.

**Her failure mode.** She tries to use the loop *against* the loop: carrying things across the night, outrunning it, staying awake through it, keeping Tuesday forever. Each of those fails in a way that points back at her, which is the game's whole thesis seen through a funhouse mirror.

**What she has that the others don't.** She notices that **every night in the game starts at 11:52 PM, the minute she read Maya's text on Monday.** The loop is hinged on the moment she turned the phone face down. A whole day is just a longer way of not turning it over.

## Endings atlas

Fifteen endings, grouped by kind. Each one names what it says about regret.

### A. New fixes that fail

#### 1. Wearing It Out
**What happens.** Nell stops trying to fix the day and starts trying to *keep* it. Every Tuesday, Maya is still in the driveway at 6:30, so she lives it on purpose, again and again, as an archive. The day starts to wear out like a tape played too many times. Theo's line skips: *"Eight forty— Eight forty—"*. At the driveway she can't remember whether the jacket sleeves were pushed up or down. One night she tries to picture Maya's face and gets the version she made up on Tuesday nine.

> Night: You try to picture her face, and you get the one you made up on Tuesday nine.
> You can’t remember which one was real.
> You played it so many times you wore the real one out.

**Chain.**
> If I can't fix it, I can at least keep it.
> But keep what? It's over.
> Not here it isn't. Here she's in the driveway every night at six-thirty.
> But it's the worst day of my life.
> It's also the last one she's in.
> So I'll just watch it. I won't change anything, I'll just watch.
> But I've watched it so many times I can't remember if her sleeves were up.
> Which one was her?

**Locks.** At least one fix tried (so she knows no version keeps Maya), and many Tuesdays (around 10 or more), or a typed intent at the morning prompt like KEEP TUESDAY or I DON'T WANT TOMORROW.
**Moments.** All seven, through "worn" lines, plus the night.
**Kind.** New fix that fails (the "archive" fix; the wanting is *preserving*).
**Why it isn't enough.** Replaying a memory doesn't keep it. It replaces it with your copies.
**Theme.** This is overthinking itself, made literal. Every replay at 2 AM edits the original a little, until you're mourning your own reconstruction.

#### 2. The Jacket
**What happens.** At the driveway Nell asks for her jacket back, the first thing she has ever asked Maya for. Maya takes it off right there and folds it, which nobody does to a jean jacket.

> “I was going to give it back at the bus.”
> “You weren’t.”
> “No.”
> She’s in a T-shirt. It’s cold by eight. She goes.

Night: *"You fall asleep in it. Somewhere on the highway she's cold, and she's got nothing of yours. You wanted something to keep. You took back the thing she was keeping."* The next morning the hook is empty.

**Chain.**
> I never asked for it back.
> I never ask for anything back. If you ask, someone can say no.
> And now it's going to Chicago without me.
> If I'd asked, at least I'd have something.
> But she'd be cold on the bus.
> I don't care. I care. It's my jacket.
> It smells like her now. That's the point.

**Locks.** None. Asking is always possible. (A fitting irony for the Nell who never asks.)
**Moments.** Driveway. The 7:14 hook reply changes afterward.
**Kind.** New fix that fails (*taking back*).
**Why it isn't enough.** You can hold the object and still lose the person. Worse: the jacket was Maya's way of taking Nell with her.
**Theme.** Holding on to things is a way of refusing to let them be given.

#### 3. The Loop, Out Loud
**What happens.** At the driveway (or the tower), Nell tells Maya the truth about the loop.

> “I’ve lived today eleven times.”
> Maya sets the box down. She doesn’t laugh. “Okay.”
> “You believe me?”
> “You knew what the box said before you looked at it.” She sits on it. “Do I go? In all of them?”
> “Every time.”
> She rubs the charcoal on her thumb against her jeans. It doesn’t come off.
> “Then that part’s mine, Nellie. Stop trying to fix my part.”
> [if why_secret] “You keep thinking if you say it right, I stay. I’m not leaving because you said it wrong.”

Night: *"She believed you. She'll never know she did. That part's hers. You keep saying it to the ceiling. That part's hers."*

**Chain.**
> Nobody knows what this is like.
> I've said goodbye to her eleven times. She's said it once.
> If she knew, she'd get why I keep getting it wrong.
> But she'd think I was crazy.
> She wouldn't. She draws the tower every day like it's alive.
> So I'll tell her.
> But what do I tell her? That she leaves every time?

**Locks.** `relived`. The second line hits harder with `why_secret`.
**Moments.** Driveway (new words: *loop, again, before, lived, eleven times, tuesday*). Optionally the tower.
**Kind.** New fix that fails (*sharing the weight*). It could count as a sixth fix, or just teach NEW `her_part`.
**Why it isn't enough.** She can't make someone else carry her regret, and Maya won't remember. What she gets is better than a fix: Maya draws the line between Nell's mistake and Maya's choice.
**Theme.** A lot of regret is taking blame for other people's decisions. Letting go starts with handing back the part that was never yours.

#### 4. The Sketchbook Page
**What happens.** Back in Maya's room (it needs `sketch`), Nell opens the inside cover again. Under *Nell — I didn't tell you because* and the black scribble, she writes in her worst handwriting: *I know why. It's okay. Go.* At the driveway the sketchbook rides on top of the box, and Maya doesn't open it.

> Night: It’s in the box. It’s going to Chicago. She’ll open it on Wednesday and—
> There isn’t a Wednesday.
> You did the thing she did. You put it in the sketchbook instead of saying it to her face.

**Chain.**
> "I didn't tell you because—" She crossed it out so hard the pen went through.
> She couldn't say it either.
> So I'll write mine under hers. She'll find it in Chicago.
> But Chicago is tomorrow, and there isn't a tomorrow.
> And anyway, that's what she did. Wrote it instead of saying it.

**Locks.** `sketch` (from `house`). Without it, the locked attempt: *"You don't know where she keeps it. You don't even know if she still draws."*
**Moments.** 7:14, going to her house a second time (a new case of `house` that needs `sketch`), then the driveway.
**Kind.** New fix that fails (*the letter*).
**Why it isn't enough.** Writing it down is the same avoidance Maya regrets in the epilogue (*"I kept putting it in my sketchbook instead of saying it to your face"*). And nothing written inside the loop can reach a future the loop doesn't have.
**Theme.** You can't mail anything to tomorrow from inside a regret.

#### 5. Eleven, Ten, Nine (the shrinking town)
**What happens.** The existing `stay` branch already has *"The streets are shorter on the walk home. Eleven streets. Ten. Nine."* The dreamer follows that line. Every Tuesday she hides or keeps Maya, Calder loses a street. The church is missing at 3:15, and she doesn't remember not passing it. Eventually:

> 3:15 PM. You walk. Your house. Dot’s. The tower. Your house.
> 6:30 PM. The Reyeses’ driveway isn’t there. There’s the end of your street, and then the end of your street again.
> Night: Calder is one street now. It’s yours. Nothing changed. There’s nothing left to change.
> The stain on the ceiling is the whole map.

The next morning, all eleven streets are back. *"You count them twice."*

**Chain.**
> If she stays, nothing has to change.
> If I stay in bed, nothing has to happen.
> But things keep happening. Theo. The box. The bus.
> So I'll go to fewer places. Skip the diner. Skip Main Street.
> If I don't go anywhere, nothing goes wrong anywhere.
> But then where am I?

**Locks.** `keep`, plus `hide` taken twice (or any two hides). It needs a street counter.
**Moments.** Street, driveway, and the `hide`/`stay` nights.
**Kind.** New fix that fails (*avoiding*). The darkest ending here.
**Why it isn't enough.** Avoidance works perfectly. It just leaves you nowhere, not even in the driveway where you could fail.
**Theme.** Regret makes the world exactly as small as the fear. *"The time loop is the town made literal,"* followed to its end.

### B. Learning scenes

#### 6. The Window Seat
**What happens.** After the station fix, at 8:40 she rides the bus again (STAY ON THE BUS, WATCH THE WINDOW). This time she doesn't sleep. The bus circles, and the road takes it back through the day.

> It’s light out, somehow. It’s 11:40. Through Dot’s window there’s a girl in booth four with her hands flat on the table, like the table might float off.
> 3:15. The same girl, stopped at the corner, looking up at the ladder like it’s a thousand feet.
> 6:30. The girl in the driveway. From here you can see her face. She looks so scared. She looks like somebody about to do a thing she’ll think about for the rest of her life.
> You put your hand on the glass. She doesn’t look up.
> She’s seventeen. She doesn’t know anything yet. She’s doing her best, and her best is *Fine. Then go.*
> Maya, asleep on your shoulder, says something you don’t catch.

Night: *"You can't warn her. You couldn't then. But you could stop yelling at her."*

**Chain.**
> I should have warned myself.
> But I can't. I'm the only one here who knows.
> If I could just see her, me, that morning, I'd tell her: scroll up. Look at the counter. Go up the ladder.
> But you never see it from inside. You'd have to be outside it.
> The bus goes around the whole town. It goes past everything.
> So I'll stay on.

**Locks.** `station` (she has to know the bus circles). It teaches NEW `window_seat`.

**Why it works.** It's the beat the game is missing. Every fix is aimed at Maya. This one is aimed at Nell's past self, and it isn't a fix at all. It's tenderness.
**Theme.** You can't reach the person you were. You can only look at her kindly. It also sets up the epilogue's "like it happened to somebody else": this is the first time she sees herself as somebody else.

#### 7. The Ring
**What happens.** At 8:40 she climbs the tower instead of running for the bus. At 8:52 the bus pulls out past the feed store. She watches the taillights go down the highway, bend, keep bending, and come back into Calder past WELCOME TO CALDER.

> From up here you can see the whole shape of it. Eleven streets and the road around them.
> It’s the shape of the stain on your ceiling.
> It was never Ohio.

**Chain.**
> The bus goes around. The road goes around. I've ridden it.
> But from inside, you can't see the shape.
> From the tower you can see everything. That's why she goes up there.
> If I could see the shape, I'd see the way out.
> But what if the shape is just… me?

**Locks.** None to try it. Before the way out is open it always plays this way. It teaches NEW `ring`, which changes the 7:14 stain reply and the driveway "which state" reply. (`station` makes it land, but it isn't required.)
**Moments.** 8:40 PM, plus free replies at 7:14 and the driveway.
**Kind.** Learning scene.
**Why it isn't enough.** Seeing the loop isn't leaving it. But it tells her where the loop is: not in Calder. In her.
**Theme.** Once you can name the shape of your loop, it starts to be just a shape. It pays off the Wednesday line already in the game: *"The stain on the ceiling is just a stain."*

#### 8. Who's Telling This
**What happens.** In felt mode, after `nobody_laughed`, Nell argues with the warped text at the driveway (SHE ISN'T LAUGHING, THAT'S NOT WHAT HAPPENED, LOOK AT HER FACE).

> ~ She laughs. A small, mean laugh you’ve never—
> No.
> Nobody at the diner was laughing either, and you’d have sworn on your life.
> You make yourself look at her face instead of the jacket.
> Her eyes are red. Her chin is doing the thing it did at her grandmother’s funeral.
> “Say something.”

A free reply anywhere, WHO'S TELLING THIS: *"Just you. You've been telling it to yourself since last night, like it happened to somebody else."*

**Chain.**
> She laughed at me.
> But nobody at the diner was laughing either, and I'd have sworn on my life.
> So what else did I get wrong?
> Who even tells it like this? The streetlight moving. The pies staring.
> It's me. It's always been me, telling it.

**Locks.** `nobody_laughed` (from `breathe` or `theo`). It teaches NEW `her_face`, which switches the driveway from felt to heal before the tower. It doesn't use the day.
**Moments.** Driveway, plus the free reply everywhere.
**Kind.** Learning scene, inside a moment that stays open.
**Why it isn't enough.** She still doesn't know *why*. She only knows she can't trust how she remembers it.
**Theme.** Memory lies to protect you, and it picks the version that hurts more and blames you less. Catching it lying is the first loosening of the grip. It also seeds the epilogue's reveal that the narrator is Nell, without spoiling it.

#### 9. The Practice Run
**What happens.** In the kitchen, with `mom` and `feelings` known.

> “Friends who aren’t what, Mom.”
> “Who aren’t leaving.”
> “That’s not what you were going to say.”
> She looks at the coffee maker.
> Tomorrow is Tuesday. She won’t remember any of this. You can say anything.
> “Friends who aren’t girls I’m in love with.”
> The coffee maker finishes. It beeps three times. Nobody moves.
> Then she puts her keys down on the counter, which she never does, because she always loses them. She comes back. She kisses the top of your head, and this time she stays there, chin on your hair, long enough to be late. She is never late.
> “Eat your eggs,” she says, into your hair.
> They’re cold. You eat them.

Night: *"She won't remember. Tomorrow she'll ask the coffee maker again. You will, though. You said it out loud in the kitchen, and the kitchen is still there."*

(Mom still never finishes her sentence. Nell finishes her own.)

**Chain.**
> Mom almost said it. "Friends who aren't—"
> But she didn't finish, so I didn't have to.
> I could finish it for her.
> But then it'd be said. Out loud. In our kitchen. Forever.
> Except it wouldn't be forever. Tomorrow's Tuesday. She won't remember.
> So I can say anything.

**Locks.** `mom` (she has heard the unfinished sentence) and `feelings`. The locked attempt, with only `mom`: *"Friends who aren't what, Mom." "Who aren't leaving." You let her have it. You don't have anything to trade.* It teaches NEW `out_loud`.
**Moments.** Kitchen. The new words (*gay, in love, girls, finish, aren't what*) need to win over `confess`, which is aimed at Maya.
**Kind.** Learning scene, or "other": rehearsal.
**Why it isn't enough.** It resets for Mom, and the driveway is untouched. But it's the one thing from the loop Nell can actually carry into Wednesday: she's said it once, and she lived.
**Theme.** The loop can't change the past, but it can change you. That's the forward half of the thesis.

#### 10. Read 8:49

**Kind.** Learning scene.
**Why it isn't enough.** It's empathy, not repair. Maya can't answer from a moving bus, and Nell gets to feel that silence, not fix it.
**Theme.** Regret softens when you feel the other side of it. It pays off the epilogue's *"I watched it say Read."*

#### 11. Midnight
**What happens.** At night, she refuses to sleep.

> 11:52. 11:53. You count. You hold your eyes open with your thumbs like a cartoon.
> 11:59.
> The phone, face up for once, says WED 12:00 AM.
> For one second it’s there.
> Then the alarm. 7:14. You never blinked.

The next morning, one extra line: *"It's there. Wednesday. It's been there the whole time, just on the other side of something you haven't done yet."*

**Chain.**

> So I won't fall asleep.
> If I don't sleep, it can't be tomorrow, and it can't be Tuesday again either.

**Locks.** None (needs `relived`). It teaches NEW `wednesday`.
**Moments.** The night. It needs the night to accept one input.
**Kind.** Learning scene, and a small fix that fails (*refusing the ending*).
**Why it isn't enough.** You can't hold a day open by not closing your eyes. It ends whether you watch or not.
**Theme.** Wednesday exists. That's the hope the game otherwise withholds until the very end, given once, early, as a glimpse.

#### 12. Only True Things Keep
**What happens.** Every night she writes instructions on her palm: SCROLL UP, DON'T SAY FINE, KNOCK. Every morning her palm is clean. Then one night she writes something that's simply true: SHE TRIED.

> Your palm is clean. Of course it is.
> [the night she writes a true thing, the next morning] There’s ink on your palm. Faint, like it’s been through the wash.
> SHE TRIED.
> It’s the only thing that ever made it through a night.

**Chain.**
> I keep making the same mistake because my mouth gets there before I do.
> But I remember everything. That's the problem.
> So I'll put it where my body can see it. On my hand.
> But the ink's gone in the morning. All of it.
> Everything I write to fix it is gone in the morning.
> Except the one that wasn't a fix.

**Locks.** Writing an instruction always fails. Writing a truth needs that fact (`tried_to_tell`, `read`, `feelings`, `why_secret`), and the palm shows whichever one she wrote.
**Moments.** 7:14 or 8:40 (or the night, if it opens), plus the next morning's first lines.
**Kind.** Learning mechanic, and a THINK the story can see.
**Why it isn't enough.** She can't send herself instructions. She's left with what she knows, which the loop was already keeping.
**Theme.** This is the game's real rule (*"The day resets. What you've learned doesn't."*) told as a story: the past keeps what you learned, never what you meant to do differently.

#### 13. The Porch Light
**What happens.** At the porch she doesn't knock. She waits to find out who decides when the light goes off.

> At 7:58 the porch light clicks off. Through the gap in the curtain you see whose hand it is.
> Hers.
> She stood there the whole time with her hand on the switch, waiting for the knock.

**Chain.**
> If I'd knocked, she'd have opened it. I know that now.
> But I didn't know then. The light just went off.
> Somebody turned it off. Somebody decided I wasn't coming.
> Was it her?
> I'd only know if I stayed and watched.

**Locks.** None, but it needs a different input from ENTER (WAIT, WATCH THE LIGHT, STAY UNTIL IT GOES OFF). It teaches NEW `porch_light`.
**Moments.** Porch.
**Kind.** Learning scene, **learned by letting it happen.**
**Why it works.** It's the only learning scene where Nell changes nothing and still finds out the truth. She practices letting go inside a learning scene.
**Theme.** Some things you can only see when you stop trying to change them.

### C. New ways out and epilogues

#### 14. The Corkboard of Tuesdays
**What happens.** Every fix leaves a photo-booth strip on the corkboard that was never taken. The tower, four frames of Maya laughing. The driveway, kissing, the flash too late. A bus window, both asleep. Booth four, Maya not drawing. Once the way out is open, at 7:14 she can TAKE THEM DOWN.

> You unpin them one at a time. You don’t look at them while you do it, because you’d keep one.
> When you’re done, the corkboard is what it was on Monday. The last strip. Fish face, fish face, fish face, and her looking at you instead of the camera.
> That one happened.

Then the honest day plays as it does now.

**Chain.**
> Every time I fix it, it's gone in the morning.
> But it happened. I was there. I kissed her. I knocked. I got on the bus.
> Where do those days go?
> They have to go somewhere.
> There. On the corkboard. A new strip every morning.
> But only one of them is real.

**Locks.** Each strip is tagged by its fix (`peace`, `keep`, `confess`, `sorry`, `station`). Taking them down needs `ready`.
**Moments.** 7:14 (free reply, tagged lines), then the existing honest day.
**Kind.** A new way out: a ritual that opens the true ending.
**Why it works.** The better versions crowd out the real one. Taking them down is choosing the day that happened over the days she wanted.
**Theme.** Letting go means taking down the versions you made up. It turns *"It was good the way a photograph is good"* (the peace night) into a physical object.

#### 15. Taillights
**What happens.** Once the way out is open, on the honest day, at 8:40 she climbs the tower to watch the bus go instead of lying on the covers. This time the taillights don't bend back.

> They go down the highway and keep going. They get smaller. They get to be the size of a star, and then they aren’t even that.
> You watch the place where they were for a long time.
> Then you climb down. The ladder is cold. It’s just a ladder.

Then the final night, with one line swapped: *"Somewhere out on the highway, the bus is getting smaller"* becomes *"You watched it go. All the way."*

**Chain.**
> Somewhere out on the highway, the bus is getting smaller.
> Every night I hear it. I've never seen it.
> I've never once watched it actually go.
> If I watched it go, maybe it would be gone.
> But I'd have to watch the whole thing, till there's nothing.
> The tower's the only place high enough.

**Locks.** `ready`, and ideally `ring` (so she has seen it bend back before).
**Moments.** 8:40 PM, then the final night.
**Kind.** New way out, a variant of the true ending. It counts as letting the seventh moment happen, because she doesn't stop the bus. She witnesses it.
**Why it works.** Every other night she hears the leaving with her eyes closed. Witnessing an ending all the way through is what lets it end.
**Theme.** Acceptance isn't looking away. It's watching it go.

**Design question for the author.** Does climbing the tower break "let every moment happen"? The dreamer's argument: the original Nell lay on the covers so she *wouldn't* see. Watching isn't changing what happens to Maya. It's changing what Nell does with her own eyes. If that feels like cheating, use this as the final night's image instead and leave the input out.
